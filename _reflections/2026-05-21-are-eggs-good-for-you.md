### From Scientific Journals to Popular Headlines: What Gets Lost in Translation

![Social media post claiming that eggs protect your brain](/assets/images/eggs.jpeg)

*We’ve all seen Facebook posts making these kinds of claims. How true are they? Source: [The Science Pulse](https://www.facebook.com/thesciencepulsee/)*
{: .caption}

A few weeks ago, a friend sent me a social media post claiming that eating more than one egg per week reduced Alzheimer’s risk by 47%. They asked me whether it was true.

So I did what researchers are trained to do: I looked for the original study. What I want to check is whether the findings actually align with how they’re presented in headlines. Actually, if I’m honest, the thing I check for first is whether there even is such a paper making this claim. In this case, the paper was real, so that’s a great start!

In [their 2024 paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S002231662400289X?via%3Dihub) published in the Journal of Nutrition, Pan and colleagues examined whether egg consumption among older adults was associated with later risk of Alzheimer’s. The study followed around 1,000 participants with an average age of 80 years old. Participants reported their egg consumption through a one-time food frequency questionnaire and were then followed up about 7 years later to assess whether they developed Alzheimer’s disease.

The researchers found that participants who ate eggs more frequently appeared to have a lower risk of developing Alzheimer’s dementia during the follow-up period. Based on these findings, the authors concluded that higher egg consumption may be associated with lower Alzheimer’s risk, potentially partly through nutrients such as choline that are important for brain health.

I have to say, I’m a little cautious about how far we can take these findings. [Alzheimer’s is a condition that develops over decades](https://www.alz.org/alzheimers-dementia/what-is-alzheimers), often well before the age of 80, so I’m not sure we can readily extrapolate these findings to a broader population. To be fair, this may not be a flaw of the study so much as a limitation of what the study was designed to answer.

I reached out to my colleague [Dr Natalia Ortega](https://projects.unifr.ch/pophealthlab/?page_id=1416), a nutritrional epidemiologist at Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Harvard Medical School, for her perspective. One of the first things she pointed out was deceptively simple: what are we comparing?

As Natalia explains, “*In this case, eating 1 egg per week compared to what? When we introduce a change in our diet, we do it by substituting one food for another given that our calorie consumption tends to be stable. In this study, no explicit substitution is made. This means that they could be both comparing eating 1 egg per week versus instead of that egg, a slice of pizza, or a couple of apples, which has potentially very different effects. Additionally, the total calorie intake must be part of the covariate or adjustment set they enumerate.*” In other words, eating more eggs might look very different depending on whether those eggs are replacing ultra-processed foods or replacing fruits and vegetables, for example.

Natalia also echoed my concern, “*about the ability to recall how many eggs a week we ate on average in the last year, asking only once in a food frequency questionnaire.*” She then added, “*But I worry mostly about this ability on participants without Alzheimer’s at 80 who will develop it. It would be better to rely on multiple questionnaires in different years or questionnaires and other tools such as food diaries or 24-h dietary recalls.*”

These kinds of methodological limitations are common in nutrition research, and indeed across health research. But communicating them clearly in short media articles or social media posts is difficult, and important nuances can easily be lost in the process.

So, are eggs actually good for you?

The thing is, scientific studies are not designed to answer broad questions like “are eggs good for you?”. They answer much narrower ones: among a specific population, over a specific period of time, under specific conditions, is egg consumption associated with a particular outcome? Different studies ask different questions, which is why headlines can sometimes appear contradictory.

None of this means that scientific research is unreliable. It just means that no single study can answer broad questions on its own. Instead, researchers look across multiple studies addressing similar questions and synthesize available evidence to provide a more comprehensive understanding, producing [systematic reviews and meta-analyses](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TLvF80WIXX8). These approaches still need to account for differences in study design, populations and contexts.

Even then, we will get it wrong sometimes. I can think of a few medical ideas that were once considered entirely rational that we now know to be misguided or even harmful. For about 3000 years, practices like [blood letting were considered a competely valid way to cure diseases](https://bcmj.org/premise/history-bloodletting). And there was a time, not too long ago, when people thought that you could [acquire obesity through inhaling the odor of beef](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1121911/). But willingness to revise, question and overturn previous knowledge is precisely what makes science valuable.

Scientists are trained to question, to doubt and to remain open to being wrong. As physicist Richard Feynman put it, “[All scientific knowledge is uncertain](https://maps.unomaha.community/Maher/GEOL1010/lecture1/feynman-1.pdf)…you have to leave the door to the unknown ajar. You have to permit the possibility that you do not have it exactly right.”

Scientific knowledge isn’t static. When the answers seem to change, it is not a sign that science is broken. It is a sign that it is working as intended.
